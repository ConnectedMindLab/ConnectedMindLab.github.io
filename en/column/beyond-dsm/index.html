Beyond the DSM — Understanding and Training Psychopathology Through a Cognitive Model

Jul 2, 2026

1. The Utility and Limits of Diagnostic Systems

The current standard in mental health clinical practice is the DSM (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders). The DSM classifies mental disorders based on the type, frequency, and duration of symptoms, and it holds clear practical value for communication among clinicians and for research categorization.

However, the DSM has structural limitations. The most fundamental problem is its categorical approach. The DSM draws a binary line — if a certain number of symptoms are present, the disorder is “present”; if not, it is “absent.” Yet in actual clinical practice, psychopathology is distributed along a continuum. Difficulties in attentional regulation, vulnerability in emotional regulation, and cognitive rigidity all form a spectrum regardless of diagnostic status.

The second problem is comorbidity. Receiving a single diagnosis in clinical settings is the exception rather than the rule. Between 60 and 80 percent of children with ADHD receive one or more comorbid diagnoses (Pliszka, 2015), and the comorbidity rate between anxiety disorders and depressive disorders exceeds 50 percent. This suggests that DSM categories do not align with actual neurobiological boundaries.

The third problem is the absence of mechanism. The DSM describes what appears, but does not explain why or how it appears. A diagnostic label is a description of a phenomenon, not an explanation of the mechanisms that generate it.

2. RDoC: A Mechanism-Centered Alternative Classification System

The most systematic response to these limitations is the RDoC (Research Domain Criteria), proposed by the NIMH (National Institute of Mental Health) in 2010 (Insel et al., 2010).

RDoC rests on two core premises. First, psychopathology must be understood along a continuum of brain and behavioral function. Second, research on mental disorders should be organized around functional domains, not DSM diagnostic categories.

RDoC proposes five domains:

  • Negative Valence Systems: responses to fear, threat, and loss — the core of anxiety and trauma-related pathology
  • Positive Valence Systems: reward seeking, motivation, and pleasure — the mechanisms underlying depression and anhedonia
  • Cognitive Systems: attention, working memory, cognitive control, and perception — the core of ADHD and executive function disorders
  • Social Processes: social cognition, representations of others, and attachment — the mechanisms underlying autism spectrum conditions
  • Arousal/Regulatory Systems: sleep, arousal levels, and autonomic regulation — the common substrate across nearly all psychopathology

RDoC is clinically meaningful precisely because it explicitly acknowledges that the same mechanistic dysfunction can appear across multiple diagnoses. Deficits in attentional regulation are not unique to ADHD; they are commonly observed across anxiety disorders, depressive disorders, PTSD, and schizophrenia spectrum conditions.

3. The Transdiagnostic Perspective: Shared Cognitive Mechanisms

The concerns motivating RDoC converge with the transdiagnostic approach in clinical psychology. The transdiagnostic perspective focuses on cognitive and emotional processes that operate across multiple diagnostic categories.

Harvey et al. (2004) identified common cognitive processes that are repeatedly confirmed across a range of mental disorders. The most representative are as follows.

Rumination: repetitive, non-productive thinking about past events or negative emotions. Most extensively studied in depressive disorders (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991), rumination also operates as a core maintenance mechanism in anxiety disorders, PTSD, and social anxiety. Neuroscientifically, it is associated with failure of top-down regulation in the prefrontal–amygdala circuit.

Impaired Cognitive Flexibility: reduced capacity for attention set-shifting and cognitive reappraisal. This commonly contributes to executive function difficulties in ADHD, rigid thought patterns in OCD, and repetitive behaviors in autism spectrum conditions. This capacity is directly linked to the function of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC).

Attentional Bias: selective allocation of attention toward threat-relevant stimuli. Most prominent in anxiety disorders; in ADHD, maladaptive attentional capture by salient stimuli is observed.

Emotion Regulation Capacity: the ability to recognize and modulate internal emotional states. The physiological foundation of this capacity lies in autonomic nervous system flexibility — particularly the capacity of the parasympathetic nervous system for inhibitory control, indexed by heart rate variability (HRV) as a measure of vagal tone.

4. Aaron Beck’s Cognitive Model and Its Extensions

The most systematic elaboration of the cognitive model at the individual level is Aaron Beck’s cognitive therapy theory (Beck, 1979). Beck proposed that emotional disorders are maintained by content-specific dysfunctional cognitive schemas. In depression, the negative cognitive triad regarding the self, the world, and the future is central; in anxiety disorders, the overestimation of threat and underestimation of coping ability are key.

Clark and Beck (2010) integrated these into a Unified Cognitive Theory of Anxiety, explaining within a single framework the cognitive-emotional processing that operates across the various subtypes of anxiety. Central to this theory is the imbalance between automatic processing and elaborative processing of threat-relevant information.

This perspective carries important implications for cognitive training. Dysfunctional schemas are not fixed entities; they are processing patterns. Processing patterns are shaped by experience and can therefore be changed through systematic intervention.

5. Functional Profiling: From Measurement to Training

The clinical approach at ConnectedMind Cognitive Science Institute is grounded in the theoretical framework above. We do not begin with a diagnostic label; we begin with an individual’s cognitive functional profile.

Profiling involves quantitative measurement across the following domains.

Attentional System: sustained attention, selective attention, and shifting attention are measured through the Comprehensive Attention Test (CAT) and computerized tasks.

Working Memory: auditory and visual working memory capacity and manipulation efficiency are assessed. These are directly linked to learning efficiency and executive function.

Executive Function: inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, and planning ability are evaluated. Deficits in this domain appear commonly across ADHD, anxiety, and depression.

Autonomic Regulation: heart rate variability (HRV) serves as an index of vagal tone and reflects the physiological foundation of emotion regulation capacity. Low HRV is associated with difficulties in emotional regulation, anxiety, and attentional instability (Thayer & Lane, 2009).

EEG Patterns: quantitative EEG (QEEG) measures activation patterns, band power ratios, and connectivity across brain regions. For example, elevated frontal theta/beta ratios are consistently associated with attentional regulation difficulties.

When these data are synthesized, it becomes clear which domains represent strengths and where functional difficulties arise. Training is then directed precisely at those functional vulnerabilities.

6. Mechanistic Rationale for Training Methods

Neurofeedback: real-time EEG feedback trains the individual to voluntarily regulate brain activation patterns. It is grounded in principles of operant conditioning and neuroplasticity. Frontal alpha/theta training enhances the balance between cognitive relaxation and focused attention; theta/beta protocols are applied to attentional regulation and impulse inhibition.

HRV Biofeedback: resonance frequency breathing (approximately 0.1 Hz, 5–6 breaths per minute) trains cardiovascular synchronization in the autonomic nervous system (baroreflex sensitivity). Enhanced vagal tone strengthens top-down regulation between the prefrontal cortex and the amygdala, directly improving the physiological basis of emotion regulation.

Transcutaneous Vagus Nerve Stimulation (taVNS): low-current stimulation delivered to the auricle of the ear activates the locus coeruleus in the brainstem via the afferent pathway of the vagus nerve. This modulates norepinephrine release, influencing arousal levels, attention, and working memory. It is non-invasive and an accumulating body of evidence supports its role in enhancing cognitive function.

Computer-Based Cognitive Training (CBT / Sensory Integration Training): adaptive tasks targeting specific cognitive functions repeatedly activate the relevant circuits. The scope of near-transfer and far-transfer effects depends on task design and training intensity.

7. Conclusion: From Diagnosis to Function

A DSM diagnosis is a starting point for clinical conversation. But it should not become the map for treatment. Diagnostic categories do not align with the brain’s actual functional boundaries; multiple distinct mechanisms can coexist within a single diagnostic label.

The cognitive science approach asks: How does this person’s attentional system function? Is the physiological foundation of emotional regulation sufficiently flexible? Which cognitive processes are maintaining the current difficulties?

When these questions are answered, the direction of training becomes clear. ConnectedMind Cognitive Science Institute designs individualized cognitive training on the basis of these principles.
